Reflection on the course

Before BAENG 101 I believed that knowing grammar was the same as communicating well. The first few weeks showed me the difference. The course treats English as a tool with a purpose: to inform, to request, to persuade or to present. Each activity had a real audience in mind, and that changed how I prepared.

The session I remember most is the group discussion on whether YouTube is a reliable source of income. I opened by defining what "reliable" should mean, consistency, stability and long-term sustainability, and then argued for the motion with examples of creators who have turned the platform into full-time careers. It stayed with me because the room pushed back well: on algorithm changes, on sponsors walking away, on how fast trends fade. I had to defend a position in real time instead of from notes, and by the end I agreed with some of the other side.

Under Saraswathy ma'am's guidance I learnt to open a discussion by framing the terms, so the whole group argues about the same thing, and to concede a good point without giving up my own. I also noticed that the quietest members had the sharpest observations when they were given room, which changed how I think about leading a discussion.

By the end of the semester I want this portfolio to show a clear line from where I started to where I have reached, in both writing and speaking.

Assignment 04Read my group discussion summary

Topic: Is YouTube a reliable source of income?

The group was divided on the topic, but both sides presented thoughtful arguments supported by logic, research and real-world examples. MrBeast's remarkable success was referenced repeatedly throughout the discussion as a benchmark for what the platform can offer.

I opened the discussion by defining what makes an income source "reliable" and the criteria it should satisfy, such as consistency, stability and long-term sustainability. As I supported the motion, I argued that YouTube is a credible career path, and backed my position with examples of several creators who have built successful, full-time careers on the platform. The discussion was balanced, and every member had an equal opportunity to present their views.

Some members highlighted the risks of depending on YouTube as a primary source of livelihood, while others explained how creators and their teams generate revenue beyond ad income, through brand deals, sponsorships and merchandise. We also compared the security of a traditional 9-to-5 job with the flexibility and uncertainty of full-time content creation.

The debate also went beyond finances. Several members examined how a creator's personal identity and beliefs can shape their income: openly sharing opinions can build a loyal community, but it can also alienate sponsors or parts of the audience. We also discussed how YouTube's algorithm heavily controls a channel's visibility, so even a small change in how videos are recommended can cause views, and therefore earnings, to drop suddenly. Finally, we considered how audience preferences shift over time, as trends fade quickly and creators must constantly adapt to stay relevant. Many of us agreed that these factors make YouTube income less predictable than it appears.

To conclude, each of us was asked what kind of channel we would start ourselves. The ideas were diverse: some suggested educational channels on history, mathematics or physics; one member chose sports content; another proposed documenting their journey of learning new skills; and one member suggested vlogging everyday life at VIT.
